Good writing is good thinking

October 29, 2026

The product we deliver is the result of one person’s thinking, or a whole team’s.

Why solve this problem? Who comes first? What deserves our time, and what can wait? Those judgments all end up in the product. A user may never read our documents, but they will encounter our choices as they use what we make.

That is why I increasingly see writing as part of making a product.

An idea can feel complete while it stays in my head. A few keywords, an intuition, and some unspoken assumptions seem to fit together. When I try to write a paragraph, the gaps appear. Whose problem is this? Why solve it now? Why would this approach work?

The place where I struggle to write is often the place I have not understood. More adjectives do not help. I need to go back, work it out, and try again. That process changes the idea. Sometimes it persuades me to drop it.

Take “help people build healthy habits.” It sounds like a direction. To turn it into a product, I have to keep going. Someone has twenty minutes today and cannot finish their planned workout. Do we ask them to make up for it later, or help them find something they can finish now? Answering those questions gives the product a shape.

What matters to me is whether I understand more after writing than I did before. A document can read smoothly and still avoid the difficult choices. A rough paragraph that lays out a problem and its tradeoffs can already move a discussion forward.

This matters even more in a team. Everyone can agree on a direction while imagining very different things. Once I write it down, someone else can point out a flaw, add a fact I did not know, or ask a question I missed. An individual idea can become a judgment the team understands and accepts responsibility for.

Working with coding agents makes this especially clear to me. A vague request can quickly become a working page. Only when I see it do I notice behavior I never properly considered. Whatever I leave unexplained will still find its way into the product somehow.

Turning some of my working methods into AI skills is the same exercise. When I write instructions for another person or an agent, I have to explain steps I once took for granted. Why do this? When does it not apply? How do we judge the result? The method itself becomes clearer as I write.

Of course, a proposal can make sense on paper and still solve a problem nobody has. Writing puts my judgments where I can examine them. Real use has to test them. New facts should change both the idea and the product.

I want to write well because I want to understand things more clearly. Other people will live with those thoughts through the products they use. That deserves another round of thought before we deliver.
