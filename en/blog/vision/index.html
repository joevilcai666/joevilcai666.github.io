Vision

August 4, 2026

If you want to build products, learn to dream first.

As children, we readily talk about what we want to become. As adults, we can feel awkward about wishes. Execution, efficiency, and progress sound more practical. But the more I think about starting a business, the more I value the ability to imagine a life I want to live, or a product that does not yet exist.

A business has a concrete requirement: make something valuable enough that someone will give you their hard-earned money for it. It has to solve a need. Revenue also has to cover costs if the business is to continue.

That is difficult. Often, the difficulty begins before the work does: what problem do I actually want to solve?

One approach is to observe other people. Friends, an industry, or a particular kind of company can all reveal unmet needs. You can look for opportunities there. But in my own experience, I have also found it difficult to become someone else. Knowing that a person has a problem is a long way from understanding how it shapes their life.

For me, a more credible starting point is something I need myself.

Some things happen every day, and you have had enough of them. Plenty of tools might already exist, but none works the way you want. You can explain what feels wrong, why it feels wrong, and how you wish it worked. A vision grows out of that distance between reality and something you want to bring closer.

That is part of why I am building Origin. After I started working, the things that did not deserve my continued attention, but still had to be handled, kept taking up my time. I want a product to take on that work so people can save their attention for things they care about. That wish came before a feature list. It also helps me decide which features belong.

Starting with my own problem gives me a reason to do the work. I can use the product repeatedly and feel whether it makes life a little better, rather than only trying to infer that from someone else’s description.

Of course, my need is only a starting point. It does not prove that other people share it, or that they will pay to solve it. The product still has to meet actual use, choices, and rejection. The value I imagine has to become value someone else can feel.

But the need to test an outcome does not make the wish dispensable. Without a direction I care about, faster execution can become a way of chasing the next plausible opportunity.

I want to answer a few questions honestly first. What have I had enough of? What do I wish existed instead? If nothing available does the job, am I willing to make it?

Learning to dream is something I want to learn before I build.
